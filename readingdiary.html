Books that I have been reading

I want to keep track of my reading, and have created this reading diary to do so. This is, in part, a way to encourage myself to finish books I start, especially while I still have the free time to read entire books. The kick-off date for this diary is 1 August 2026, and I plan to update it irregularly.

Sputnik Sweetheart by Haruki Murakami — August 2026

Japanese lesbians just get so lonesome, I could cry. I read this book on holiday with my dad sitting by Lake Annecy in the garden of the Abbaye de Talloires.

Dying Animal by Phillip Roth — August 2026

Beautiful book about a dirty old man. I bought this book in Geneva, when I had 6 hours to kill there and didn't want to talk to any Swiss. I finished it on the way back on the aeroplane, sometimes worried that people would read over my shoulder.

The Napoleon of Notting Hill by G.K. Chesterton — August 2026

Bizarre old book, which I read on the sofa of Thames Rowing Club on a hot day.

England, England by Julian Barnes — August 2026

Very strong first chapter, but let down by extended Euro-satire that feels irrelevant now.

The Map and the Territory by Michel Houellebecq — September 2026

Very fun read, and another book that touches on the subject of dirty old men - in this case, Michel Houellebecq himself.

Fire on the Moon by Norman Mailer — September 2026

Absolutely ridiculous piece of writing - some wonderful detail (including about how ice-cold the skin of the Saturn V was). The usual complaint remains - the Apollo astronauts were boring.

One Hundred Letters from Hugh Trevor-Roper, edited by Richard Davenport-Hines and Adam Sisman — October 2026

Bitchy, pompous whiggery permeates. HTR is a rather fun, if dislikable, character in writing. Fantastic piece of correspondence features HTR inquiring about the nature of the Peterhouse 'school': "When I, timidly, ask such questions in Peterhouse, the answer (like all answers from that quarter) is oracular but opaque. However, I begin to detect, through the fog, some faint lineaments of a definition. For instance, I learn that the Peterhouse historians are all philosophic historians, and tory historians (they regard me as a ‘Whig’—an Anglican Whig, says Cowling; an infidel Scottish Whig, says Worsthorne). I also learn that their founding fathers are the philosopher Michael Oakeshott (why is he not honoured by the Crown, cries Worsthorne; ‘nothing less than an O.M. will do’) and the historian Butterfield. Now I have to admit that I have not read much of either of these gurus—only enough to convince myself that they are not worth reading. In this I may well be wrong. But I am prejudiced against Butterfield by seeing his legacy in Peterhouse—and also the historical mafia which, by his patronage, he created there and elsewhere, largely in Ireland (an academic colony of Peterhouse). To Peterhouse he gave the genius of Cowling; to Dublin that of Desmond Williams. Another member of the mafia is Brian Wormald. Cowling has some pupils whom he tries to force on us, but none of them have written anything to indicate their character, except a young man called David Wootton whom Cowling sent to me as a research student in Oxford and then tried to force on Peterhouse as a Fellow; but the Papists stood firm against him, being a Marxist (Cowling has declared, in his book, his own opinion that Marxism is ‘right but unimportant’). Now what is the common denominator of this ‘school’? They—or rather those of them who can utter—seem to me to have some common qualities. First, they are all ideologues in search of a dogma. Butterfield was a Methodist tub-preacher. Cowling is a resentful inner émigré from Anglicanism. Wormald is a convert to popery. Secondly, their writing is always opaque and circular and seldom finished—which hardly matters since it has no direction. Butterfield rambles round his Methodist tub. Wormald (as Noël Annan put it) ‘wrote half a book, backwards’. Cowling is writing an open-ended part-work of which vol. 1 — the only one in print — leads nowhere.

John Maynard Keynes: A Biography. Vol. 1, Hopes Betrayed, 1883-1920 by Robert Skidelsky — October 2026

Great short section on the re-emergence of the universities as places of serious thought in the late 19th century. Interesting section on Keynes' career at the India Office.

Before She Met Me by Julian Barnes — October 2026

About a man whose life is ruined by an amusing, but at times terrifyingly recognisable, neurosis about his wife having committed 'past-adultery' with her previous lovers before she met him. Draws out an interesting idea about the destructiveness of jealousy.

The Ruling Caste: Imperial Lives in the Victorian Raj by David Gilmour — October 2026

A fantastic evocation of the lives of young men who arrived in India with the ambition to govern it, with almost no life experience behind them. The sections I enjoyed most (for obvious reasons) focus on their transition from the safety of university and Haileybury into the exhilarating unknown of life on a strange continent.
